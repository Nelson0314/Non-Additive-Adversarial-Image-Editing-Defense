\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Recent generative image models such as Stable Diffusion~\cite{rombach2022ldm} and the instruction-guided editors built on them~\cite{brooks2023instructpix2pix,alekseenko2026vibe,bfl2026flux2klein} allow a publicly released photograph, portraits in particular, to be modified with a text prompt. This capability raises privacy and security concerns. Image immunization~\cite{salman2023photoguard} addresses them on the side of the image owner, who modifies a photograph before release so that edits of the released copy diverge from edits of the original. PhotoGuard introduced this setting for Stable Diffusion image-to-image editing and inpainting. Subsequent work extended it to instruction-guided editing~\cite{dctshield2025}, inpainting under attacker-chosen masks~\cite{dia2025}, and facial identity~\cite{wang2025facelock}.

Most of these methods share one form. They superimpose an additive adversarial perturbation $\delta$ on the image, optimized under an $\ell_\infty$ or $\ell_2$ budget with projected gradient descent against a component of the editor~\cite{salman2023photoguard,liang2023mist,dia2025,dctshield2025}. The perturbation is set independently at each pixel and forms a pattern tied to the pixel grid of the original photograph. Methods designed for robustness keep the additive form and change where $\delta$ is placed. DCT-Shield~\cite{dctshield2025} perturbs quantized JPEG coefficients, BlurGuard~\cite{blurguard2025} applies region-wise Gaussian blur to $\delta$ to bring its spectrum closer to that of natural images, and SRIM~\cite{srim2026} optimizes $\delta$ against the weakest of several input scales so that it survives the rescaling applied by editors.

This spatial structure makes the perturbation fragile. Purification operations that an attacker can apply before editing remove it. JPEG compression~\cite{sandoval2023jpeg} and noisy upscaling~\cite{honig2025noisyupscaling} bypass several protections, and VAE-based purification removes the protection of six methods without access to the protected images or to the defense~\cite{purifyonce2026}. Contrastive adversarial training of a lightweight adapter removes it on the side of the editor~\cite{cat2025}. Evaluations of edit outcomes report that protected images are often still edited as instructed~\cite{icip2025disruptive} and that protections fail under chains of common transformations~\cite{doprotect2026}.
We propose \capg{}, a filter-like defense that encodes the protection in the colors of the image. Each image receives its own 3D color lookup table (LUT), the operator of global color grading~\cite{zeng2020lut}. Acting on colors alone, the LUT introduces no spatial structure into the image (\cref{sec:carrier}), and the perturbation it applies is preserved by purification (\cref{sec:exp_purify}). The protected image appears as a color-graded version of the original. Color mappings have served as adversarial carriers against image classifiers~\cite{laidlaw2019functional,zhao2023advcf,yuan2022ncf}. We bring this family of transformations to immunization against diffusion editing.

A LUT optimized without restriction can produce banding, unnatural skin tones, and colored fringes in neutral regions. We restrict the form of the mapping instead of penalizing each artifact. The LUT rotates each color vector about the black point, with the gray axis as the polar axis. The polar angle, which governs saturation, and the azimuth, which governs hue, each change within a small bound, and the rotation varies smoothly across color space (\cref{sec:feasible}). Every mapping in this family is a smooth color shift, and the two angle bounds control both the quality of the protected image and the size of the perturbation.

We evaluate what purification preserves at three levels. The first is the perturbation itself: the fraction of the difference between the protected and the original image that remains after a purification (\cref{fig:purification}). The second is the edit: the LPIPS between the edit of the protected image and the edit of the original, which we call edit displacement (\cref{tab:main}). Before purification, the edit displacement of a defense follows its distortion, the LPIPS between the protected and the original image (\cref{fig:plane}), so defenses of different forms are compared at matched distortion. The third is the outcome: the rate at which an edit that succeeds on the original image fails on the protected one (\cref{tab:isr}). For \capg{}, all three are unchanged by purification, whereas for additive defenses all three decrease; before purification, the rate of failed edits of \capg{} is not higher than that of the additive defenses.

Full-reference metrics such as LPIPS measure the difference from the original image, so any change of color increases them. We therefore report the LPIPS of the protected images together with the no-reference aesthetic models LAION and MUSIQ, which score the protected image itself.

Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item We show that a perturbation applied to the colors of an image carries no spatial structure and is preserved by purification, whereas additive perturbations are removed: after VAE-based purification, 0.83 of the perturbation of \capg{} remains, against 0.03 to 0.35 for ten additive defenses (\cref{sec:exp_purify}).
  \item We propose \capg{}, which realizes such a perturbation as a per-image 3D LUT restricted to smooth, bounded rotations of color about the gray axis, so that the protected image is a color-graded version of the original (\cref{sec:feasible}).
  \item We evaluate on 306 portraits against ten additive defenses at matched distortion, under seven standard purifications and VAE-based purification. On a 100-portrait subset, the rate at which a requested edit fails, judged by Claude Sonnet~5.5 with human review of a sample of the judgments, is 17.0\% for \capg{} without purification and 16.5\% over three purifications (JPEG compression, blur, and VAE-based purification), against 2.6\% to 12.2\% for the additive defenses after purification (\cref{sec:exp_isr}).
\end{itemize}
